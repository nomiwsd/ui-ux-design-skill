# Age-Inclusive Design

A 6-year-old, a 17-year-old, a 35-year-old, and a 75-year-old are not the same interface with different content. Motor skills, reading ability, patience, and trust signals genuinely differ. Treat the audience's age range as a first-class input from discovery.

## Children (3–12), split by developmental stage

**Ages 3–5 (pre-literate)**
- Text is close to meaningless: lead with icons, illustration, and voice narration.
- Immediate audio/visual feedback on every tap.
- Very large, forgiving touch targets (60×80px minimum).
- No fine-motor gestures — no pinch-zoom, no precision dragging.
- No time pressure, no losing states.

**Ages 6–8**
- Simple reading is emerging: short words, active voice, icons paired with labels.
- Light gamification (stars, simple rewards) works well.
- Flat navigation, 1–2 levels deep maximum.

**Ages 9–12 (tweens)**
- Can handle real navigation and decision-making, and actively dislike being talked down to.
- Still need instant feedback and forgiving, non-punishing error states.

**Cross-cutting rules**
- Never put primary buttons at the very bottom edge — small hands mis-tap.
- Ads must be clearly distinguishable from content; children cannot reliably tell the difference.
- Parental gates before purchases, external links, or data sharing.
- Design for two audiences at once: the child using it and the parent who must trust and approve it.
- COPPA (US), the UK Age Appropriate Design Code, and equivalent regional rules are not optional.

## Teens and young adults (13–25)

- High tech fluency; this group notices and penalizes anything that reads as "designed for kids" or visually dated.
- Bold typography, high-contrast dark themes, and trend-forward styles (glass, motion, neubrutalism) land specifically well here.
- Very short patience for onboarding — get to value in seconds, make tutorials skippable.
- Social proof and peer validation (reviews, UGC, sharing) outweigh formal trust badges.
- For 13–17, many regions require distinct data-privacy handling once a product knows or suspects a minor user.

## Adults (26–59)

- The widest variance in tech literacy of any bracket — persona research matters *most* here, because "adult" alone tells you almost nothing.
- Usually task-focused (work, finance, shopping): prioritize speed-to-task and clarity over decorative delight.
- A light/dark choice is now a baseline expectation, not a bonus.
- Assume interruption and multi-tasking; preserve state, autosave, make it easy to resume.

## Seniors (60+)

The barrier is almost never willingness to use technology. It is design that ignores real physiological change.

- **Vision**: roughly 1 in 6 adults over 70 report vision impairment. Larger base font sizes with user-adjustable scaling, high contrast, and never color alone to convey meaning.
- **Motor control**: larger touch targets and more generous spacing than the general adult baseline; avoid precise dragging, multi-finger gestures, and fast timed interactions. Avoid hover-only reveals entirely.
- **Cognitive load**: shallow, consistent navigation; labelled icons rather than abstract symbols alone; fewer choices per screen; help that is easy to find rather than hidden behind a "?".
- **Trust and error recovery**: plain-language errors with no codes or jargon, confirmation before irreversible actions, and generous undo. Preventing the feeling of "I broke something" is the single biggest lever against abandonment in this group.
- Mild skeuomorphic cues (a trash-can for delete, a physical-looking toggle) map to real-world experience more intuitively than fully abstract flat icons.
- Reduce motion by default; vestibular sensitivity increases with age.

## Products spanning a wide age range

Don't try to please every age with one interface. The tested approach: **one accessible, high-contrast, generous-target baseline that works for the oldest and least able users, plus a "simple / advanced" density toggle** that unlocks complexity for users who demonstrate they want it. Remember the setting per user, and make switching back obvious.
